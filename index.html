    Constant-rate infusion test

    Marmarou's single-compartment model: an exponential pressure–volume curve plus a linear outflow resistance. The plateau depends only on Rout; elastance sets how fast the curve gets there.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dP}{dt}=E\,(P-P_0)\Big[I_f+I_{inf}(t)-\frac{P-P_{ss}}{R_{out}}\Big]\]
    \[P_b=P_{ss}+I_fR_{out},\qquad P_{plat}=P_b+I_{inf}R_{out},\qquad \mathrm{PVI}=\frac{\ln 10}{E}\]

    Compliance \(C=1/[E(P-P_0)]\) shrinks as pressure rises, so the curve accelerates and then saturates as absorption catches up with infusion. The dashed red line marks the plateau that Rout = 18 mmHg·min/mL would produce: a plateau above it implies a resistance above the Dutch NPH Study threshold.

    Changing \(R_{out}\) here holds \(P_b\) fixed, so the implied sagittal sinus pressure \(P_{ss}=P_b-I_fR_{out}\) moves. Lumbar infusion also assumes free lumbar–intracranial communication.

    Constant-pressure infusion test

    Ekstedt's method turns the infusion test around. Instead of fixing the infusion rate and watching pressure, a servo holds CSF pressure at a series of set levels and records the flow needed to keep it there. Once each level settles, the flow equals net absorption, so the flow–pressure line gives outflow conductance (1/Rout) directly from its slope. A level set below sagittal sinus pressure stops absorption: the drained flow then equals CSF formation, and sinus pressure can be calculated.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[I_{inf}(t)=\underbrace{\frac{\max(P-P_{ss},0)}{R_{out}}-I_f}_{\text{steady part}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{E\,(P-P_0)}\frac{dP}{dt}}_{\text{compliance filling}}\]
    \[\bar I_k=C_{out}\,(P_k-P_r),\qquad R_{out}=\frac{1}{C_{out}},\qquad I_f=-\bar I_{drain},\qquad P_{ss}=P_r-I_f\,R_{out}\]

    Pressure moves to each new level with the servo time constant τs. The transient flow fills the compartment's compliance and dies away; the mean flow over the last 40% of each step is taken as steady. Linear regression of steady flow on pressure, using levels above sinus pressure, gives Cout and the resting pressure Pr where the line crosses zero flow. Ekstedt found this relationship to be rectilinear: once the arachnoid villi are open, higher pressure does not distend them further. Below sinus pressure absorption stops, so all CSF formed drains into the system.

    Compared with constant-rate infusion, Rout comes from several steady states rather than one plateau, so it does not depend on reaching a plateau or on the pressure–volume curve. The cost is a longer test and the need for a pressure-controlled pump.

    Slow vasogenic (B-wave) fluctuations are represented as correlated noise in the measured flow. Real tests also contend with pressure-dependent sinus pressure, needle resistance and lumbar–intracranial gradients, which are not modelled here.

    Fluid bolus injection (and withdrawal) test

    A small volume is injected (or withdrawn) over a second or two. The size of the immediate pressure jump reads the pressure–volume curve: the pressure–volume index (PVI) is the volume that would raise pressure tenfold. The return to baseline over the next minutes reads absorption, so the same bolus gives outflow resistance through Marmarou's formula. The test is quick but depends on the pressure the curve is referred to.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\mathrm{PVI}=\frac{\Delta V}{\log_{10}\!\big(P_p/P_0\big)},\qquad R_{out}=\frac{t\,P_0}{\mathrm{PVI}\,\log_{10}\!\Big[\dfrac{P_t}{P_p}\cdot\dfrac{P_p-P_0}{P_t-P_0}\Big]}\]

    Pressure is simulated with Marmarou's equation, injecting ΔV over the chosen time. The classic formulae assume the exponential pressure–volume curve is referred to 0 mmHg. When the true reference pressure Pref is not zero, the classic PVI is biased. The bias is upward when Pref > 0, so compliance looks better than it is. The corrected versions refer every pressure to Pref: replace P with P − Pref in both formulae. Rout follows from the logistic recovery after the peak and is independent of sinus pressure. A withdrawal (negative ΔV) gives the same PVI with less risk of a pressure wave.

    Clinically, bolus-derived Rout is typically lower than infusion-derived Rout (Shapiro 1980 reported 2.8 ± 0.8 mmHg·min/mL). In the model the two agree, because absorption is linear. The clinical gap is usually attributed to pressure-dependent resistance, vasogenic volume changes and the short observation window.

    A single compartment, linear absorption and no pulse or respiratory waves. In practice PVI is averaged over several boluses, readings are taken at end-expiration and diastole, and injections are avoided when ICP is high.

    The shunt equation: Bernoulli applied to a ventriculoperitoneal shunt

    Flow through a shunt follows the energy balance between the ventricular catheter tip and the peritoneal outlet. Bernoulli's equation is extended for viscous flow by adding the valve opening pressure and the resistive losses in the valve and catheter. When the patient sits up, the column of CSF in the shunt adds a siphoning pressure ρgh that can exceed the valve setting several times over. That is why upright overdrainage happens and why gravitational and anti-siphon devices exist. The velocity term ½ρv² is shown too: at shunt flow rates it is negligible.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[P_1+\rho g z_1+\tfrac12\rho v_1^2=P_2+\rho g z_2+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2+P_{open}+Q\,(R_{valve}+R_{cath})\]
    \[Q=\frac{\max\big(0,\;\mathrm{ICP}+\rho g H\sin\theta-P_{abd}-P_{open}(\theta)\big)}{R_{valve}+R_{cath}},\qquad R_{cath}=\frac{8\mu L}{\pi r^4},\qquad Re=\frac{\rho v D}{\mu}\]

    Point 1 is the ventricular catheter tip at ICP; point 2 is the peritoneal outlet at intra-abdominal pressure. The height difference is H sin θ for a head-up angle θ. With the same internal diameter at both ends, the velocity terms cancel. Even taken alone, ½ρv² is a few hundredths of a mmHg, because shunt flow is slow and laminar (Reynolds number in the tens to low hundreds). So in practice the "shunt equation" is a hydrostatic balance plus Hagen–Poiseuille losses. A gravitational unit adds opening pressure in proportion to sin θ, offsetting part of the siphon. The equilibrium ICP solves If = max(0, (ICP − Pss)/Rout) + Q(ICP): formation leaves by physiological absorption and by the shunt.

    Valves are idealised as an opening pressure plus a linear resistance; real differential-pressure valves have pressure-dependent resistance, hysteresis (see "Valve hysteresis") and, for flow-regulating designs, a deliberately non-linear pressure–flow curve. Catheter obstruction, distal pseudocysts and subcutaneous tunnel compression of anti-siphon devices are not modelled.

    Pressure reactivity index (PRx) and optimal CPP

    PRx is a moving correlation coefficient between slow waves of arterial pressure and ICP. When cerebral vessels react, a rise in arterial pressure constricts them, cerebral blood volume falls and ICP falls or stays put: PRx is zero or negative. When they are pressure-passive, ICP follows arterial pressure and PRx becomes positive. Plotting PRx against CPP usually gives a U-shaped curve whose minimum is the "optimal" CPP (CPPopt).     Presets

    Parameters

    Method

    \[\mathrm{PRx}_k=\operatorname{corr}\big(\overline{\mathrm{ABP}}_{k-N+1..k},\;\overline{\mathrm{ICP}}_{k-N+1..k}\big),\qquad \mathrm{CPP}_{opt}=\arg\min_{\mathrm{CPP}}\big(a+b\,\mathrm{CPP}+c\,\mathrm{CPP}^2\big)\]

    Signals are averaged over short periods (Czosnyka et al. used 5 s, Steiner et al. 6 s; 10 s is common in current software) to remove pulse and respiratory components while keeping slow waves. PRx is the Pearson correlation over the last N averages (about 3–5 minutes), recalculated every minute. For CPPopt, PRx values are grouped into CPP bins of 5 mmHg. A parabola is fitted, weighted by the number of values in each bin, and its minimum taken. CPPopt is reported only when the curve is a genuine U within the observed CPP range: the fitted minimum must be well below the curve at the edges and below 0.3. Sorrentino et al. found PRx thresholds of 0.25 for survival and 0.05 for favourable outcome, with CPP 70 mmHg and ICP 22 mmHg.

Without a time column, enter the sampling rate. Gaps (blank or non-numeric cells) are filled with the previous value.

    Overnight ICP: average pressure and percentage of B-waves

    An overnight ICP recording is summarised by its average level and its slow-wave activity. Lundberg's B-waves are rhythmic oscillations at 0.5–2 per minute, common during sleep and in hydrocephalus. Their burden is expressed as the percentage of monitoring time they occupy. That percentage depends heavily on the amplitude threshold used to define a wave, so the threshold curve is shown alongside. Use the demo recording, or load your own ICP file; it is read only inside your browser and is not uploaded.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Method

    \[x_B(t)=\overline{\mathrm{ICP}}_{10\,s}(t)-\overline{\mathrm{ICP}}_{180\,s}(t),\qquad \text{B-wave if } \max_{w}x_B-\min_{w}x_B\ge A_{thr}\ \text{and}\ 0.5\le f_w\le 2\ \text{min}^{-1}\]

    The signal is first averaged to 1-second values. A 10-second moving average removes pulse and respiratory waves, and subtracting a 180-second moving average removes slower drifts, leaving a band of roughly 30 s–3 min periods. Every 10 seconds, a 2-minute window centred on that point is classified as B-wave activity if its peak-to-peak amplitude reaches the threshold and its zero-crossing frequency lies within 0.5–2 per minute. The % B-waves is the fraction of these 10-second blocks classified as B-wave, out of the total monitoring time, as in Stephensen et al. They tested thresholds of 0.5–5 mmHg and showed how strongly the percentage depends on that choice. Mean and median ICP, spread and the time above a chosen threshold describe the average pressure.

    Automated B-wave counts vary with the filter, window, amplitude threshold and artefact handling; values are comparable only within one method. The demo recording is synthetic (B-wave trains of random period 30–120 s on a drifting baseline), so the detector can be checked against a known answer. Not for clinical decisions.

    Where the Navier–Stokes equations apply to CSF

    CSF is a Newtonian liquid with the viscosity of water, so its motion obeys the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations everywhere. The equations are rarely solved in full. Instead, two dimensionless numbers decide which terms matter at each site. The Reynolds number Re compares inertia with viscosity; the Womersley number α compares pulsation frequency with viscous diffusion across the channel. This tab places each CSF pathway on that map. It then applies the exact or reduced Navier–Stokes solution that fits each one:

    • the cerebral aqueduct, normal and stenosed;
    • an endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) stoma;
    • the cervical subarachnoid space;
    • the shunt catheter;
    • spinal CSF leaks and CSF–venous fistulae in intracranial hypotension;
    • perivascular spaces;
    • red-cell sedimentation in intraventricular haemorrhage.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\rho\Big(\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}}_{\text{unsteady}}+\underbrace{\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u}_{\text{convective}}\Big)=-\nabla p+\underbrace{\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u}_{\text{viscous}}+\rho\mathbf g,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad Re=\frac{\rho UD}{\mu},\quad \alpha=R\sqrt{\frac{\omega\rho}{\mu}}\]

    Hydrostatics (u = 0). With no flow, the equations reduce to ∇p = ρg. This is the basis of every posture effect in these models, including the upright pressure fall in intracranial hypotension.

    Steady pipe flow. In a straight uniform tube the convective term vanishes identically. The exact solution is then Hagen–Poiseuille, Q = πR⁴ΔP/(8μL), which governs the shunt catheter. It also gives an annular version for the spinal canal: Q = πG/(8μ)[(R₂⁴ − R₁⁴) − (R₂² − R₁²)²/ln(R₂/R₁)].

    Oscillatory flow. For a sinusoidal pressure gradient in a rigid tube, Womersley's exact solution is u(r,t) = Re{(G/iωρ)[1 − J₀(i3/2αr/R)/J₀(i3/2α)]eiωt}. It is used for the aqueduct. Between parallel walls the analogous cosh solution describes the cervical subarachnoid gap. When α is well above 1 the profile flattens, peaks move towards the walls and the pressure gradient leads the flow, as Loth et al. found in the spinal canal.

    Stenoses and orifices. Where the cross-section changes, the convective term returns. For a stenosis, the Young–Tsai form is ΔP = viscous + Kt(ρ/2)(A₀/A₁ − 1)²U² + unsteady, with Kt = 1.52. For a hole in a thin membrane (an ETV stoma or a dural tear), the creeping-flow limit is Sampson's exact solution ΔP = 3μQ/a³, with an orifice term (ρ/2)(Q/CdA)² (Cd = 0.62) and an inertance ρ/(2a) added.

    Creeping flow. When Re ≪ 1 the inertial terms drop out entirely, leaving Stokes flow. That describes perivascular spaces, and Stokes drag on a sphere, v = 2Δρ g r²/(9μ), gives red-cell settling. A Richardson–Zaki factor (1 − φ)4.65 corrects for haematocrit, and a cluster of n cells settles as a sphere of radius r·n1/3.

    Leaks. If the leak path can carry CSF formation at almost no pressure difference, CSF pressure at the leak settles at the downstream pressure: epidural for a dural tear, venous for a fistula. Upright, the hydrostatic column then lowers ICP at the foramen of Monro by ρgh, where h is the height of the Monro above the leak.

    These are one-dimensional or idealised exact solutions, not full three-dimensional CFD. Walls are taken as rigid, though brain and cord motion drive the flow. Sizes and stroke volumes are representative, and the coefficients Kt, Cd and the 1.2 inertance factor come from engineering correlations. The leak model ignores dural-sac compliance, venous engorgement and absorption changes, all of which soften the upright pressure fall in real intracranial hypotension. CSF viscosity is 0.7–1 mPa·s even with high protein (Bloomfield 1998). Not for clinical decisions.

    Pulse amplitude, compensatory reserve and RAP

    Each heartbeat injects a small arterial blood volume. The pressure pulse it produces is that volume multiplied by the local slope of the pressure–volume curve, so amplitude tracks position on the curve until arterial beds start to collapse.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dP}{dV}=\max\!\Big[E(P-P_0),\;\tfrac{1}{C_{max}}\Big],\qquad \mathrm{AMP}\approx \Delta V_{pulse}\,\frac{dP}{dV}\,g(\mathrm{CPP})\]

    The flat segment (compliance capped at \(C_{max}\)) gives the lower breakpoint, where RAP is near 0. Along the exponential segment AMP rises linearly with ICP and RAP approaches +1. The factor \(g(\mathrm{CPP})\) is a logistic reduction in transmitted pulse volume as cerebral arterioles collapse at low perfusion pressure; past the upper breakpoint, AMP falls as ICP rises and RAP turns negative.

    RAP is computed as the correlation between AMP and mean ICP over simulated slow fluctuations (±3 mmHg) with 3% amplitude noise, mimicking the bedside moving-window estimate.

    The P1/P2/P3 morphology is phenomenological: the tidal (P2) component is scaled with the loss of reserve to reproduce the clinical pattern, not derived from vascular mechanics.

    Bayesian estimation of Rout

    A synthetic infusion study is generated from known parameters, then the Marmarou model is fitted on a grid to give the joint posterior of Rout and E. The clinically useful output is the probability that Rout exceeds a threshold, not a point estimate.

    Data

    Model

    \[y_k=P(t_k;R_{out},E)+\varepsilon_k,\quad \varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\]
    \[\log R_{out}\sim\mathcal N(\log m,s^2),\qquad \log E\sim\mathcal N(\log 0.15,\,0.8^2)\]

    \(P_b\) is taken from the mean of the 5-minute baseline and \(P_0=0\) is assumed known. The posterior is evaluated on a coarse grid, then refined around the region holding the mass.

    Three things to try. Shorten the infusion: E becomes poorly identified and the Rout posterior is pulled towards the prior. Add B-waves: the iid likelihood is now misspecified and the posterior becomes overconfident around a biased value. Move the truth near 18: the probability of exceeding threshold carries the uncertainty that a dichotomised point estimate discards.

    With correlated physiological noise, an AR(1) or Gaussian-process error model is more honest than the iid likelihood used here.

    Cerebrovascular coupling and plateau waves

    A reduced Ursino–Lodi model. Arterial compliance is set by an autoregulation controller that responds to cerebral blood flow, and arterial blood volume sits inside the same rigid box as CSF. With low intracranial compliance and intact but strong autoregulation, a small perturbation can trigger the vasodilatory cascade.

    The restricted-pulsation hypothesis

    Each systole delivers about 1 mL of arterial blood into the skull. Normally this is accommodated by pushing CSF into the spinal canal and squeezing cortical veins. If excess CSF volume exhausts that reserve, ICP pulsates in phase with the arteries, arterial expansion is restricted, and pulsatility is pushed downstream into capillaries and veins. The hypothesis proposes that this loss of the intracranial windkessel, rather than mean pressure, produces symptoms.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    Negative-pressure hydrocephalus and mantle hysteresis

    Ventricles can stay large, and keep enlarging, while ventricular pressure is normal, low or below zero. Two features explain it. First, the ventricles and subarachnoid space become disconnected, and a leak or loss of the subarachnoid space keeps convexity pressure low, so the transmantle gradient stays positive even at a low ventricular drain height. Second, the mantle yields plastically: once stretched, it does not recoil when the gradient falls to zero.     Egnor and colleagues propose that hydrocephalus from subarachnoid obstruction is not CSF malabsorption but failure of the cerebral windkessel. Normally the pulsatile (AC) part of arterial inflow passes through the subarachnoid CSF to the veins, and the capillaries see only steady (DC) flow. Blocking the subarachnoid path redistributes pulsatility to the ventricles and capillaries. Ventricular dilation is an adaptation, and shunts act as accessory windkessels. Use the presets in order.

    A current divider at the heart-rate frequency, driven by a fixed arterial pulsatile inflow. The subarachnoid and ventricular CSF paths are series RLC branches tuned to resonance at 72 bpm, the capillary bed is resistive, and the shunt is an optional resistive branch. Normal resistances (2, 112, 150), shunt resistance (5.155) and the ventriculomegaly radius (about 1.73×) follow the paper.     The paper held voltage fixed and set each path's resistance per state to match Greitz's MRI flows, including lowering capillary resistance in the SAH state. Here a fixed inflow drives the redistribution, and inertance is scaled up so that inertia and stiffness have a visible effect. The single-node circuit cannot represent aqueductal obstruction or herniation.

    The normal cerebral windkessel, with the ventricular system

    The arterial wave is a steady (DC) part plus a pulsatile (AC) part. In systole, subarachnoid arteries swell; CSF is pushed into the spinal sac (the main route) and a little down the aqueduct, and venous outflow surges, while capillary flow stays almost steady. Block the subarachnoid space to see the pulse diverted into the ventricles and capillaries.

    Model

    An illustrative animation, not a calculation. The amplitudes of each path are set to roughly match Greitz's flow-MRI ratios: foramen magnum to aqueduct stroke volume about 56:1 normally. With subarachnoid obstruction, subarachnoid pulsatility roughly halves and aqueductal pulsatility rises about 9-fold.

    In reality cerebral capillary flow is strongly damped rather than completely pulseless.

    An ISS astronaut from the launch pad to orbit

    One astronaut's day of launch and the months that follow. Walking out to the rocket, the astronaut is upright under 1 g along the body axis (Gz). In the capsule they lie on their back, legs raised. During ascent the load runs chest to back (Gx) and builds to 3–4 g. At engine cut-off, about 9 minutes after lift-off, the load falls to zero. Over the following weeks the ventricles slowly enlarge and the subarachnoid space over the vertex narrows. Once in orbit, ICP and the CSF volumes are the same whether the astronaut is "vertical" or "upside down": with no gravity vector there is no hydrostatic column, so orientation in the cabin has no effect.

    Load is resolved into body axes. Gz acts head to foot (positive when the head is up). Gx acts chest to back, as when lying supine in the seat, so the seat-back angle β splits the rocket's sensed load into Gz = n sin β and Gx = n cos β. Sinus pressure follows the venous column between the right atrium and the head. When the head is above the heart, jugular collapse caps that column at Lv; head down, the full column Lz applies. The thoracic-loading term kx(Gx − 1) is the change relative to lying supine on Earth. Removing the weight of the chest and abdomen lowers central venous pressure: Lawley et al. measured a fall from 7 to 4 mmHg on entering 0 g from supine, with ICP falling from 17 to 13 mmHg. Higher Gx raises it. Inside the skull, CSF has its own hydrostatic gradient across the head height H in the direction of the load, so frontal and occipital pressures diverge during ascent while ICP at the foramen of Monro moves much less.

    Volume changes on the scale of minutes come from the exponential pressure–volume curve: CSF moves between the cranium and the spinal sac, and a fraction φ of the change is in the ventricles. Adaptation over weeks follows the spaceflight MRI findings: the ventricles enlarge by a fraction fv, and brain tissue expands by B mL and shifts upwards. A fraction kS of that combined expansion is taken from the convexity subarachnoid space, which narrows at the vertex. An optional rise in Rout represents the reduced CSF absorption proposed by Van Ombergen et al.

    "Vertical" vs "upside down" in orbit. On the ISS, the station and the crew fall freely together, so the only loads left are residual quasi-steady accelerations of the order of micro-g (adjustable). The pressure difference down a 12 cm head at 1 µg is about 10−5 mmHg, eight orders of magnitude smaller than pulse pressure, and it reverses sign but not size when the astronaut turns over. The inverted posture on Earth is shown for contrast. There, head-down loading fills the jugular veins and the full venous column raises ICP.

    Cabin pressure. The capsule and the ISS are held near sea-level pressure (about 101 kPa) from the pad to orbit, and the model assumes this throughout. ICP is a gauge pressure referenced to the surrounding atmosphere. A uniform change in cabin pressure is transmitted equally through the incompressible tissues, blood and CSF, so it would not change ICP or CSF volumes at steady state. Only a rapid change (decompression) or a change in gas composition matters. Cabin CO2 on the ISS has ranged from about 1 to 9 mmHg, against about 0.3 mmHg on Earth. That is a plausible additional cerebral vasodilator not included here.

    The ascent profiles are approximate and illustrative, not flight data. Thoracic venous loading under Gx, the legs-up effect, the ventricular and subarachnoid partition φ, and kS are assumptions with plausible defaults. Pressures are steady state; cerebral autoregulation, venous valves, the anti-g straining response, brain sag under Gx, optic nerve sheath compartments and the 24-hour cycle (see the "Spaceflight" tab) are not modelled. Long-duration ICP has not been measured invasively in orbit.
