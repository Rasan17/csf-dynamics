    Constant-rate infusion test

    Marmarou's single-compartment model: an exponential pressure–volume curve plus a linear outflow resistance. The plateau depends only on Rout; elastance sets how fast the curve gets there.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dP}{dt}=E\,(P-P_0)\Big[I_f+I_{inf}(t)-\frac{P-P_{ss}}{R_{out}}\Big]\]
    \[P_b=P_{ss}+I_fR_{out},\qquad P_{plat}=P_b+I_{inf}R_{out},\qquad \mathrm{PVI}=\frac{\ln 10}{E}\]

    Compliance \(C=1/[E(P-P_0)]\) shrinks as pressure rises, so the curve accelerates and then saturates as absorption catches up with infusion. The dashed red line marks the plateau that Rout = 18 mmHg·min/mL would produce: a plateau above it implies a resistance above the Dutch NPH Study threshold.

    Changing \(R_{out}\) here holds \(P_b\) fixed, so the implied sagittal sinus pressure \(P_{ss}=P_b-I_fR_{out}\) moves. Lumbar infusion also assumes free lumbar–intracranial communication.

    References

    1. Marmarou A, Shulman K, Rosende RM. A nonlinear analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid system and intracranial pressure dynamics. J Neurosurg 1978;48:332–344.
    2. Katzman R, Hussey F. A simple constant-infusion manometric test for measurement of CSF absorption. Neurology 1970;20:534–544.
    3. Boon AJW, et al. Dutch Normal-Pressure Hydrocephalus Study: prediction of outcome after shunting by resistance to outflow of CSF. J Neurosurg 1997;87:687–693.
    4. Czosnyka M, Whitehouse H, Smielewski P, Simac S, Pickard JD. Testing of cerebrospinal compensatory reserve in shunted and non-shunted patients. JNNP 1996;60:549–558.
    5. Eklund A, et al. Assessment of cerebrospinal fluid outflow resistance. Med Biol Eng Comput 2007;45:719–735.

    Pulse amplitude, compensatory reserve and RAP

    Each heartbeat injects a small arterial blood volume. The pressure pulse it produces is that volume multiplied by the local slope of the pressure–volume curve, so amplitude tracks position on the curve until arterial beds start to collapse.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dP}{dV}=\max\!\Big[E(P-P_0),\;\tfrac{1}{C_{max}}\Big],\qquad \mathrm{AMP}\approx \Delta V_{pulse}\,\frac{dP}{dV}\,g(\mathrm{CPP})\]

    The flat segment (compliance capped at \(C_{max}\)) gives the lower breakpoint, where RAP is near 0. Along the exponential segment AMP rises linearly with ICP and RAP approaches +1. The factor \(g(\mathrm{CPP})\) is a logistic reduction in transmitted pulse volume as cerebral arterioles collapse at low perfusion pressure; past the upper breakpoint, AMP falls as ICP rises and RAP turns negative.

    RAP is computed as the correlation between AMP and mean ICP over simulated slow fluctuations (±3 mmHg) with 3% amplitude noise, mimicking the bedside moving-window estimate.

    The P1/P2/P3 morphology is phenomenological: the tidal (P2) component is scaled with the loss of reserve to reproduce the clinical pattern, not derived from vascular mechanics.

    References

    1. Avezaat CJJ, van Eijndhoven JHM, Wyper DJ. Cerebrospinal fluid pulse pressure and intracranial volume–pressure relationships. JNNP 1979;42:687–700.
    2. Czosnyka M, Pickard JD. Monitoring and interpretation of intracranial pressure. JNNP 2004;75:813–821.
    3. Balestreri M, et al. Intracranial hypertension: what additional information can be derived from ICP waveform after head injury? Acta Neurochir 2004;146:131–141.
    4. Cardoso ER, Rowan JO, Galbraith S. Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid pulse wave in intracranial pressure. J Neurosurg 1983;59:817–821.

    Bayesian estimation of Rout

    A synthetic infusion study is generated from known parameters, then the Marmarou model is fitted on a grid to give the joint posterior of Rout and E. The clinically useful output is the probability that Rout exceeds a threshold, not a point estimate.

    Data

    Model

    \[y_k=P(t_k;R_{out},E)+\varepsilon_k,\quad \varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\]
    \[\log R_{out}\sim\mathcal N(\log m,s^2),\qquad \log E\sim\mathcal N(\log 0.15,\,0.8^2)\]

    \(P_b\) is taken from the mean of the 5-minute baseline and \(P_0=0\) is assumed known. The posterior is evaluated on a coarse grid, then refined around the region holding the mass.

    Three things to try. Shorten the infusion: E becomes poorly identified and the Rout posterior is pulled towards the prior. Add B-waves: the iid likelihood is now misspecified and the posterior becomes overconfident around a biased value. Move the truth near 18: the probability of exceeding threshold carries the uncertainty that a dichotomised point estimate discards.

    With correlated physiological noise, an AR(1) or Gaussian-process error model is more honest than the iid likelihood used here.

    References

    1. Gelman A, et al. Bayesian Data Analysis, 3rd ed. CRC Press, 2013.
    2. Boon AJW, et al. Dutch NPH Study: prediction of outcome by Rout. J Neurosurg 1997;87:687–693.
    3. Eklund A, et al. Assessment of cerebrospinal fluid outflow resistance. Med Biol Eng Comput 2007;45:719–735.
    4. Kennedy MC, O'Hagan A. Bayesian calibration of computer models. J R Stat Soc B 2001;63:425–464.

    Cerebrovascular coupling and plateau waves

    A reduced Ursino–Lodi model. Arterial compliance is set by an autoregulation controller that responds to cerebral blood flow, and arterial blood volume sits inside the same rigid box as CSF. With low intracranial compliance and intact but strong autoregulation, a small perturbation can trigger the vasodilatory cascade.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\Big(\frac{1}{k_E P}+C_a\Big)\frac{dP}{dt}=C_a\frac{dP_a}{dt}+\frac{dC_a}{dt}(P_a-P)+I_f+I_{inf}-\frac{P-P_{vs}}{R_{out}}\]
    \[q=\frac{P_a-P}{R_a+R_v},\quad R_a=R_{a,n}\Big(\frac{V_{a,n}}{C_a(P_a-P)}\Big)^{2},\quad \tau\frac{dx}{dt}=-x+G\frac{q-q_n}{q_n}\]

    \(C_a(x)\) is Ursino's asymmetric sigmoid: large dilatory range, smaller constrictor range. \(R_a\propto V_a^{-2}\) follows from Poiseuille flow (\(R\propto r^{-4}\), \(V\propto r^{2}\)). The venous outflow is lumped as a Starling resistor with venous pressure tracking ICP.

    The "slow MAP waves" event imposes ±5 mmHg at 60 s period and computes PRx as the correlation of 10 s means of MAP and ICP. Intact autoregulation gives negative PRx; a pressure-passive bed (G = 0) gives PRx near +1.

    This reduced model omits CO2 reactivity, capillary-dependent CSF formation and a separate venous compartment, all present in the full Ursino–Lodi model.

    References

    1. Ursino M. A mathematical study of human intracranial hydrodynamics, parts 1 and 2. Ann Biomed Eng 1988;16:379–416.
    2. Ursino M, Lodi CA. A simple mathematical model of the interaction between intracranial pressure and cerebral hemodynamics. J Appl Physiol 1997;82:1256–1269.
    3. Rosner MJ, Becker DP. Origin and evolution of plateau waves. J Neurosurg 1984;60:312–324.
    4. Czosnyka M, et al. Continuous assessment of the cerebral vasomotor reactivity in head injury. Neurosurgery 1997;41:11–19.
    5. Czosnyka M, et al. Contribution of mathematical modelling to the interpretation of bedside tests of cerebrovascular autoregulation. JNNP 1997;63:721–731.

    Posture, venous collapse and shunt siphoning

    Steady-state ICP as the head rises above the heart. Without a shunt, ICP follows sagittal sinus pressure, which is limited by jugular collapse. With a differential-pressure valve, the column of CSF in the distal catheter siphons; a gravitational unit adds opening pressure in proportion to the tilt.

    Patient and posture

    Model

    \[I_f=\max\!\Big(0,\frac{P-P_{ss}(\theta)}{R_{out}}\Big)+\frac{\max\big(0,\,P-P_{abd}+\rho g H_d\sin\theta-P_{open}(\theta)\big)}{R_{sh}}\]
    \[P_{ss}(\theta)=P_{ss,0}-\rho_b g L_v\sin\theta,\qquad P_{open}(\theta)=P_{valve}+P_{GU}\sin\theta\]

    \(L_v\) is the effective hydrostatic column between the head and the point of jugular collapse. Absorption is one-way. The equation is solved for the steady ICP by bisection at each tilt angle.

    This reduces the Qvarlander–Holmlund model to its steady state. It ignores postural changes in abdominal pressure, the vertebral venous plexus as a separate outflow, and valve hysteresis.

    References

    1. Qvarlander S, Sundström N, Malm J, Eklund A. Postural effects on intracranial pressure: modeling and clinical evaluation. J Appl Physiol 2013;115:1474–1480.
    2. Holmlund P, et al. Venous collapse regulates intracranial pressure in upright body positions. Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 2018;314:R377–R385.
    3. Petersen LG, et al. Postural influence on intracranial and cerebral perfusion pressure in ambulatory neurosurgical patients. Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 2016;310:R100–R104.

    Poroelastic cerebral mantle

    The parenchyma as a saturated porous elastic shell between ventricle and subarachnoid space. A transmantle pressure difference deforms the shell (Lamé solution) and drives Darcy flow through it; pore pressure equilibrates by consolidation over hours.

    Geometry and tissue

    Model

    \[u(a)=\frac{\Delta p\,a}{E\,(b^3-a^3)}\Big[(1-2\nu)a^3+\tfrac{1+\nu}{2}b^3\Big],\qquad Q=\frac{4\pi k\,\Delta p}{\mu\,(1/a-1/b)}\]
    \[\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=c\,\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2},\quad c=\frac{kM}{\mu},\quad M=\frac{E(1-\nu)}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)},\quad \tau=\frac{L^2}{\pi^2 c}\]

    The ventricle is represented as a sphere of equal volume. With drained moduli of order 1–2 kPa, a transmantle gradient of about 1 mmHg is enough for millimetre-scale wall displacement. This is the mechanical core of Hakim's hydraulic-press argument, and it explains why direct measurement struggles to detect the gradient.

    Linear, small-strain and isotropic. Real ventriculomegaly is large-strain, the tissue is hyperelastic and viscoelastic, and multiple-network models (MPET) separate arterial, capillary, venous and interstitial compartments.

    References

    1. Hakim S, Venegas JG, Burton JD. The physics of the cranial cavity, hydrocephalus and normal pressure hydrocephalus. Surg Neurol 1976;5:187–210.
    2. Nagashima T, et al. Biomechanics of hydrocephalus: a new theoretical model. Neurosurgery 1987;21:898–904.
    3. Tully B, Ventikos Y. Cerebral water transport using multiple-network poroelastic theory: application to normal pressure hydrocephalus. J Fluid Mech 2011;667:188–215.
    4. Penn RD, et al. Pressure gradients in the brain in an experimental model of hydrocephalus. J Neurosurg 2005;102:1069–1075.

    Perivascular solute transport: diffusion, dispersion or flow?

    A tracer enters at x = 0 and is carried along a perivascular channel or through the extracellular space. Compare pure diffusion, diffusion enhanced by oscillatory (Taylor) dispersion, and net advection. The Péclet number decides which mechanism dominates over the distance of interest.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}=D_{eff}\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2},\qquad D^{*}=\frac{D}{\lambda^2},\qquad D_{eff}=D^{*}\Big[1+\frac{(Uh/D^{*})^2}{420}\Big]\]
    \[\frac{c}{c_0}=\tfrac12\Big[\operatorname{erfc}\frac{x-vt}{2\sqrt{D_{eff}t}}+e^{vx/D_{eff}}\operatorname{erfc}\frac{x+vt}{2\sqrt{D_{eff}t}}\Big],\qquad \mathrm{Pe}=\frac{vL}{D^{*}}\]

    The exact Ogata–Banks solution is evaluated with a scaled erfc, so there is no numerical diffusion. The dispersion term is a Taylor–Aris-type enhancement for oscillatory flow in a narrow gap, using half of the steady plane-Poiseuille coefficient (1/210) for the cycle average. It is an order-of-magnitude device, not a resolved oscillatory solution.

    Set λ ≈ 1.6 and v ≈ 0 to represent the parenchymal extracellular space. Measured perivascular velocities (~20 μm/s) come from pial surface arteries in mice; penetrating and capillary-level flows remain disputed.

    Normal pressure hydrocephalus

    Three linked mechanisms explain the clinical paradox. Outflow resistance is high, yet mean ICP stays normal because formation falls with age. Elastance is raised, so pulse amplitude is high. A softened mantle lets a small, pulse-driven transmantle force enlarge the ventricles (Hakim's hydraulic press). Shunting lowers pressure and pulsatility, but remodelled tissue does not fully recoil.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\bar P = P_{ss}+I_fR_{out},\qquad \mathrm{AMP}=\Delta V_{pulse}\,E\,(\bar P-P_0),\qquad \Delta p_{tm}=\kappa\,\mathrm{AMP}\]
    \[\frac{da}{d(\Delta p)}=\frac{a\,G(a,b,\nu)}{E_b(1+\beta\varepsilon)},\qquad G=\frac{(1-2\nu)a^3+\tfrac{1+\nu}{2}b^3}{b^3-a^3}\]

    The ventricle is integrated as a sphere of equal volume under incrementally applied transmantle pressure, with strain stiffening (β = 3). Because \(G\) rises steeply as \(a\to b\), larger ventricles need less additional pressure to enlarge further: the positive feedback Hakim described. The transmantle force is taken as proportional to pulse amplitude (Bering's pulsatile hypothesis). Evans' index is estimated from volume with an empirical power law.

    The driver of ventriculomegaly in iNPH is unresolved; κ is a free parameter, not a measured constant. Evans' index mapping is approximate. The post-shunt ventricle combines elastic recoil with an adjustable irreversible (remodelled) fraction.

    References

    1. Adams RD, Fisher CM, Hakim S, Ojemann RG, Sweet WH. Symptomatic occult hydrocephalus with "normal" cerebrospinal-fluid pressure. N Engl J Med 1965;273:117–126.
    2. Hakim S, Venegas JG, Burton JD. The physics of the cranial cavity, hydrocephalus and normal pressure hydrocephalus. Surg Neurol 1976;5:187–210.
    3. Bering EA. Circulation of the cerebrospinal fluid: demonstration of the choroid plexuses as the generator of the force for flow of fluid and ventricular enlargement. J Neurosurg 1962;19:405–413.
    4. Silverberg GD, Mayo M, Saul T, Rubenstein E, McGuire D. Alzheimer's disease, normal-pressure hydrocephalus, and senescent changes in CSF circulatory physiology: a hypothesis. Lancet Neurol 2003;2:506–511.

    CSF production, flow and absorption as a circuit

    Six fluid compartments joined by resistances, fed by choroidal and extrachoroidal sources and drained by five absorption routes. Narrow a foramen or raise sinus pressure and the steady-state pressures redistribute; a pressure difference between ventricles and convexity subarachnoid space is the signature of obstructive hydrocephalus.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\sum_{j}\frac{P_i-P_j}{R_{ij}}+\sum_k G_k\max\!\big(0,P_i-P_{ref,k}-P_{open,k}\big)=I_i,\qquad R_{ij}=\frac{8\mu L}{\pi r^4}\]

    Channel resistances follow Poiseuille's law, so resistance scales with r−4: halving the aqueductal radius multiplies its resistance sixteen-fold. Normal channel resistances are tiny (the aqueduct contributes about 0.01 mmHg·min/mL), which is why steady bulk flow needs almost no pressure difference until the lumen is nearly occluded. The steady state is found by Newton iteration on the piecewise-linear nodal equations.

    Default absorption split: granulations 40%, cribriform/nasal lymphatics 25%, meningeal lymphatics 10%, spinal 15%, parenchymal 10%; extrachoroidal production 30%. These fractions are uncertain in humans and vary by species, so treat them as adjustable assumptions.

    Only mean bulk flow is modelled. Pulsatile to-and-fro flow is an order of magnitude larger: aqueductal stroke volume is tens of microlitres per beat, against a net flow of about 0.3 mL/min.

    CSF dynamics through a night's sleep

    A standard hypnogram drives cerebral blood volume (falling in slow-wave sleep, rising in REM with phasic bursts) and a circadian rise in CSF formation peaking around 02:00. The same blood-volume changes are applied to three craniospinal systems: normal compliance, untreated hydrocephalus with high elastance and Rout, and the same patient with a differential-pressure shunt.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dP}{dt}=E(P-P_0)\Big[I_f(t)+\frac{dV_b}{dt}-\frac{P-P_{ss}}{R_{out}}-I_{sh}(P)\Big],\qquad \tau_b\frac{dV_b}{dt}=V_b^{*}(\text{stage})-V_b\]

    Each sleep stage sets a target blood volume \(V_b^{*}\), approached with a 2-minute time constant. REM adds phasic bursts of about 0.6 mL. Formation follows \(I_f(t)=\bar I_f[1+A\cos(2\pi(t-2\,\mathrm h)/24\,\mathrm h)]\). The glymphatic index weights time in each stage by relative tracer influx (N3 highest, REM low), following the rodent data.

    The key teaching point is that the same 2–3 mL REM blood-volume surge produces a trivial ICP rise on the flat part of the pressure–volume curve and a REM plateau-like wave on the steep part. A working shunt clips these waves, because any rise above valve opening pressure is drained.

    The stage-specific blood-volume changes are representative, not measured in this population. Human glymphatic data across sleep stages remain indirect; the clearance index is qualitative.

    CSF dynamics across the lifespan

    Formation, CSF volume, outflow resistance, pressure–volume index and normal ICP all change with age. Turnover (daily formation divided by CSF volume) falls from about four to five exchanges a day in childhood to about one in the very old, and the same volume load is buffered very differently at each age.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\text{turnover}=\frac{1440\,I_f(\text{age})}{V_{CSF}(\text{age})}\ \text{per day},\qquad E(\text{age})=\frac{\ln 10}{\mathrm{PVI}(\text{age})},\qquad P_{ss}=\bar P_n-I_fR_{out}\]

    Smooth curves are fitted to published anchor points. Formation rises to about 0.37 mL/min in childhood and falls to about 0.19 mL/min by the late seventies. PVI scales with neuraxis volume, from about 8 mL in infants to about 25 mL in adults. Rout rises slowly after middle age. The volume-load simulation runs the Marmarou equation with each age's parameters: a constant infusion scaled to CSF volume (1.5 mL/min in an adult) for 20 minutes.

    The open fontanelle and expanding skull in infancy add compliance that grows head circumference rather than ICP; this is not modelled. Neonatal formation data are sparse and the infant curve is extrapolated.

    The restricted-pulsation hypothesis

    Each systole delivers about 1 mL of arterial blood into the skull. Normally this is accommodated by pushing CSF into the spinal canal and squeezing cortical veins. If excess CSF volume exhausts that reserve, ICP pulsates in phase with the arteries, arterial expansion is restricted, and pulsatility is pushed downstream into capillaries and veins. The hypothesis proposes that this loss of the intracranial windkessel, rather than mean pressure, produces symptoms.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[C_{sp}=C_{sp,0}\,e^{-\Delta V_{CSF}/V_r},\qquad C_v=C_{v,0}\,e^{-\Delta V_{CSF}/V_r}\]

    A linear five-node circuit is solved in the frequency domain for the first 12 cardiac harmonics. Nodes are distal arteries (compliance \(C_a\) referenced to ICP), capillaries, collapsible cortical veins (\(C_v\), referenced to ICP), the intracranial CSF space, and the spinal sac (\(C_{sp}\), reached through foramen magnum resistance and inertance). Arterial pressure upstream of a proximal resistance is the forcing input. Excess CSF volume shrinks both reserve compliances exponentially, mirroring the exponential pressure–volume curve.

    The model reproduces the direction of the predicted changes: ICP pulse amplitude rises, spinal CSF stroke volume and arterial expansion fall, and capillary and venous flow pulsatility rise. Removing a modest volume (the tap test preset) restores much of the reserve because of the exponential form.

    This is a hypothesis, not established mechanism. Measured arterial and capillary pulsatility in iNPH are inconsistent between studies, causality between pulsatility and symptoms is unproven, and the model has no ventricular compartment, so it cannot show the hyperdynamic aqueductal flow seen on phase-contrast MRI.

    Why acute, but not chronic, hydrocephalus shows periventricular lucency

    When outflow is blocked, choroidal CSF must leave the ventricles somehow. If the obstruction develops in hours, the mantle cannot yield and alternative clearance has not developed, so the only escape is across the ependyma into white matter. Interstitial water rises and CT attenuation falls. If it develops over months, slow ventricular enlargement and adaptive clearance keep pace, the transmantle gradient never builds, and the white matter stays near normal density.

    Presets

    Parameters

    Model

    \[\frac{dV}{dt}=I_{ch}-\frac{\Delta P}{R_{aq}(t)}-G\,\Delta P,\quad \Delta P=E_v(V-V_r),\quad G=\frac{L_{ep}K_c}{L_{ep}+K_c}\]

    Here \(p_t=\frac{L_{ep}}{L_{ep}+K_c}\Delta P\) is the periventricular interstitial pressure excess and \(L_{ep}\) is transependymal conductance. \(K_c\) is interstitial clearance into capillaries and perivascular routes, which increases with sustained load over the adaptation time \(\tau_a\). The ventricle yields plastically over \(\tau_c\) through remodelling. Global ICP rises exponentially with the elastically displaced volume. The system is integrated with implicit Euler on a logarithmic time grid, from minutes to a year.

    The core result is that the gradient, not the ventricular size, sets the lucency. Steady interstitial water is \(\approx\alpha I_{ch}/K_c\) when transependymal flow is the only exit, so it is large before adaptation and small after it. Slow onset lets adaptation and yielding keep pace, so the gradient never develops.

    The adaptation term is a hypothesis standing in for several observed changes: subependymal gliosis, choroid plexus atrophy, alternative absorption routes and periventricular tissue loss. The HU conversion uses about 2.5 HU per 1% water (reported range about 1.8 to 2.6).

    Pulsatile flow through the aqueduct of Sylvius

    Systolic brain expansion squeezes the ventricles and drives CSF caudally through the aqueduct; it returns in diastole. Net flow per beat is tiny, but the oscillation is large. Flow is solved as Womersley flow in a rigid tube, so you can see how radius, stroke volume and heart rate shape the velocity profile, the pressure difference needed, and the stroke volume measured by phase-contrast MRI.

    Model

    Pulsatile flow through an endoscopic third ventriculostomy

    A stoma in the floor of the third ventricle adds a short, wide parallel pathway to the prepontine cistern. Because its impedance is far lower than a stenosed aqueduct's, it takes almost the entire pulsatile stroke volume, and the ventricular pulse pressure collapses. A visible flow jet through the floor on phase-contrast MRI is the marker of a patent stoma. As the stoma closes, flow and jet velocity fall and ventricular pulsation returns.

    Decompressive craniectomy and CSF dynamics

    Removing a bone flap adds a large, nonlinear compliance: the skin flap. The pressure–volume curve flattens, oedema raises ICP far less, and the pulse amplitude falls. Once swelling settles, atmospheric pressure acts directly on the brain: sitting up or draining CSF makes the flap sink, the basis of the syndrome of the trephined and paradoxical herniation.

    After IVH or trauma: protein, osmotic gradients and the redistribution of brain water

    Blood in the CSF raises protein (oncotic pressure) and, as it lyses, osmolality. Water is drawn from the extracellular space across the ependyma and pia into CSF, while protein clogs absorption and raises outflow resistance. The CSF space and ventricles expand as the extracellular space shrinks. Cells shrink only if the extracellular fluid becomes hyperosmolar, which happens when small osmoles leak in from CSF or water leaves through a solute-reflecting barrier.

    On Earth, ICP falls every time we sit or stand, because hydrostatic columns and jugular collapse lower venous outflow pressure. In microgravity there is no upright posture.     CSF pressure and the cerebral venous Starling resistor

    Bridging veins and the lateral lacunae cross the subarachnoid space before entering the sinuses. Their thin walls are exposed to CSF pressure, so they behave as a collapsible segment: when ICP exceeds sinus pressure they narrow until upstream venous pressure sits just above ICP. CSF pressure, not sinus pressure, then sets the effective downstream pressure for cerebral blood flow. This is the vascular waterfall that couples cortical venous pressure to ICP and isolates the brain from jugular pressure changes.

    Parameters

    Model

    \[q=\frac{\mathrm{MAP}-P_{eff}}{R_a+R_c+R_{cv}+R_{bv}},\qquad P_{eff}=\operatorname{smax}\big(\mathrm{ICP}+\Delta P_o,\;P_{ss}\big),\qquad P_{ss}=\mathrm{CVP}+P_{jug}+q\,R_{jug}\]
    \[\frac{dP}{dt}=E(P-P_0)\Big[I_f+I_{ext}-\frac{P-P_{ss}}{R_{out}}+\frac{dV_{ven}}{dt}\Big],\qquad V_{ven}=C_v\,(P_{cv}-\mathrm{ICP})\]

    The collapsible segment is represented by a smooth maximum: flow is governed by MAP − (ICP + ΔPo) when the segment is collapsed and by MAP − Pss when it is open. In the dynamic scenarios, venous blood volume follows transmural pressure and adds to the Marmarou equation. This is why jugular compression raises ICP (the Queckenstedt test) only once sinus pressure approaches ICP, and why CSF drainage lowers cortical venous pressure.

    Shunt valve hysteresis: opening, closing and operating pressures

    A differential-pressure valve does not open and close at the same pressure. Static friction and seat adhesion must be overcome to lift the ball or membrane, so it opens above its nominal setting. Once moving, kinetic friction acts against the direction of motion, so flow at a given pressure is higher on the way down than on the way up. When nearly closed, the pressure acts on the smaller seat area and the valve snaps shut. In a patient with little absorption, this produces intermittent, sawtooth drainage.

    Parameters

    \[P_{open}=P_{set}+P_s,\qquad P_{close}=P_{set}-P_k+P_{snap},\qquad \text{closes when } w\le P_{snap}\]

In the patient, the valve sits between a Marmarou compartment and the peritoneum, with an optional siphon column.

    Negative-pressure hydrocephalus and mantle hysteresis

    Ventricles can stay large, and keep enlarging, while ventricular pressure is normal, low or below zero. Two features explain it. First, the ventricles and subarachnoid space become disconnected, and a leak or loss of the subarachnoid space keeps convexity pressure low, so the transmantle gradient stays positive even at a low ventricular drain height. Second, the mantle yields plastically: once stretched, it does not recoil when the gradient falls to zero. Only a sustained negative transmantle gradient (a sub-zero drain, sealing the leak, neck wraps) remodels it back. Afterwards the ventricles hold their new size at ordinary drain heights. That is hysteresis.

    Obstruction at the foramen magnum (Chiari I, arachnoiditis) disturbs the craniospinal exchange of CSF on every heartbeat and every cough. Four proposed mechanisms are computed side by side: craniospinal pressure dissociation (Williams), the tonsillar piston and altered cervical pulse (Oldfield and Heiss), the Venturi pressure drop across the narrowing (Greitz), and perivascular pumping into the cord, which depends on the timing of the subarachnoid pulse relative to arterial opening of perivascular spaces (Stoodley and Bilston).
